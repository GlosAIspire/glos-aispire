Why Glos AIspire exists

Women are adopting AI more slowly than men, and the gap matters. The largest study so far pooled 76 surveys from more than 100 countries and found around 48% of men using generative AI against 39% of women.1 Women make up under a third of the world’s AI talent.2 And as these tools learn from the people using them, a mostly male set of users pulls them further from women’s perspectives over time.

In the UK it is women’s jobs that are most exposed to automation, because we hold most of the admin and clerical roles these tools are best at.3 We’re also the ones carrying most of the mental load, which makes us the people who stand to gain most from handing the repetitive stuff to a machine. That’s the whole idea: not AI for its own sake, but time reclaimed.

Sources. 1. Cranney, Delecourt & Koning, “Global Evidence on Gender Gaps and Generative AI Over Time”, Harvard Business School Working Paper 25-023 (rev. May 2026): 47.8% of men against 39.3% of women, across 318,924 respondents. 2. Stanford HAI, AI Index Report 2026: women are 30.5% of global AI talent with AI engineering skills. 3. Greater London Authority, 2026: 8% of women working in London are in the roles most exposed to AI automation, against 4% of men. See also IPPR, 2024, on UK back-office and part-time work.

What happens at a meeting

We meet online for an hour. It usually opens with one of us showing a tool she has actually been using: ten minutes or so, in plain language, covering what it does and where it let her down. Nobody prepares slides, and nobody is expected to be an expert.

The rest of the hour belongs to whoever has brought something with her: a real problem from her work or her life that could do with a few more heads on it. We talk it through together, and people tend to leave with one thing worth trying. Themes alternate between work and home, because AI is useful in both.

    What we worked on
    One member showed us a summer of building: two websites, a paid upgrade to a quiz she already runs, and a full online course made from a book she had written. No developer, no agency. She talked an AI through all of it in a browser tab. Which raised the harder question. If a tool can reach your calendar, your inbox and every open tab, how do you choose who to trust with it? We did not agree, and that was the useful part.
    The bit we tried live
    We each asked the AI we personally pay for to rank the big providers on responsible AI, then compared answers in the room. Every one placed itself politely mid-table or better. One refused to name a winner at all. And the model topping two separate lists was one most of us had never heard of.
    What we’d recommend
    • Run that ranking prompt in your own tool this week. It takes five minutes and tells you something about what you are already paying for.
    • Tell it to stop agreeing with you. “Don’t agree with me. Find the errors in my thinking” improves the answers more than any clever prompt.
    • Use it on work you could mark yourself. If you can’t tell a good answer from a plausible one, you are not ready to let it do that job unwatched.
    • If you give an assistant access to your browser, give it one job at a time and shut the sensitive tabs first.
